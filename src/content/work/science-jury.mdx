<Section>
  <SectionLead label="Constraints and pivots" />

  <Split>
    <SplitHeading>What forced us to adapt, and what I learned from it.</SplitHeading>

    This project ran from October to December, leaving us with two months to design, build, and run preliminary user studies. **Our project lead needed the paper to defend her PhD thesis before starting an industry job.** That timeline shaped every decision about the system.
  </Split>

  <Accordion>
    <AccordionItem title="Simulating real people → Referencing their published work" subtitle="An ethics-driven pivot to preserved scholarly grounding and remove identity impersonation.">
      An early concept of our system had agents directly emulate named researchers. Authors could select an HCI professor they admired, and the agent would generate feedback in that person’s voice, drawing on their published positions.

      The more I sat with this concept, the more I felt that it was crossing an ethical line. Generating feedback on behalf of a real, named individual, without their consent, risks putting words in their mouth and misrepresenting their views. I flagged my concern in a team sync, to which everyone quickly agreed to pivot before investing more time into defining the persona logic.

      The alternative logic I proposed is a model where agents reference a person's published literature as a knowledge source but use generic role-based names ("HCI Expert," "Methods Specialist"). This preserves the grounding in real scholarly expertise without speaking on behalf of any specific person. It's a meaningful distinction that I believe was the right call both ethically and for long-term trust in the system.
    </AccordionItem>

    <AccordionItem title="Google Docs → Quill.js" subtitle="Technical constraints led us to adopt friction in exchange for richer interaction model.">
      We initially scoped ScienceJury as a Google Docs plug-in, but the Docs API doesn't support text-anchored inline comments on the front-end, a hard requirement for our contextual decomposition model.

      We pivoted to Quill.js, a rich text editor that supports location-anchored highlights and bidirectional linking. This unlocked the design, but came with a tradeoff: instead of meeting researchers where they already work, they now have to upload their document (PDF or Word) into our system. That added friction is a real adoption barrier, one I'd address in future iterations through a browser extension or native editor integrations as APIs evolve.
    </AccordionItem>

    <AccordionItem title="Tight timeline → Lean system" subtitle="Reducing the scope for clarity and testability under timeline pressure.">
      We had a longer list of features on the table, including inter-agent debate, where agents could respond to each other's critiques directly. With two months on the clock, most of it got cut.

      The decision framework was straightforward: does this feature directly serve one of the three design goals, and can we build and test it in time? Inter-agent debate was compelling and novel, but risked adding another layer of noise on top of a system. The result was a tighter, more testable system, and participant feedback confirmed that the features we shipped were the right ones to prioritize.
    </AccordionItem>
  </Accordion>
